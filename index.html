A note on the calendar

The Lead

The Tax Survived Its Lawsuit. The City Mentioned It on a Survey Page.

Ballots went into the mail October 5. Measure BB is printed exactly as the city wrote it, because a judge said it could be, on September 3, in a ruling Pacifica never announced. The campaign to pass a permanent $5-million-a-year tax has not yet reported raising $2,000. The campaign against it does not exist on paper at all.

Today is the county's deadline to mail sample ballots. The city's measure on them is the one the City Council adopted 5–0 on August 5 — the “Pacifica City Services Measure,” a one-cent sales tax, “approximately $5,000,000 annually until ended by voters.” It is the same question two Pacifica voters went to court in August to have rewritten.

The suit, Dan Stegink, et al. v. Sarah Coffey, et al., was filed August 17. In No. 4 we had its caption and its party list but not its contents. The Tribune, which read the petition (backdrop: August 25), reported that it called the question “written to sound persuasive, rather than neutral,” argued that the city's polling had tested the very phrases that ended up in it, and attacked the dollar figure: the city's existing one-percent sales tax, the petition said, “has generated between roughly $2.7 million and $2.9 million a year over the past five fiscal years.” It asked the court to make the ballot say about $2.9 million. Petitioners' counsel was Jason A. Bezis, who is also representing Stegink in a separate challenge to the regional transit measure.

Here is the city's entire public account of how that ended, verbatim, as it appears on the “Pacifica Discussion 2026” page in the City Manager's section of the website — the page that hosts the community survey:

“September 3rd Statement by the City of Pacifica Regarding Litigation Ruling. The City of Pacifica was a party to a legal challenge to Measure BB, the Pacifica City Services placed on the November 3rd Municipal Election Ballot. Judge Mark McCannon ruled in the City's favor today, denying all relief to Petitioners.”

That is the whole of it. There was no press release; the city has issued six in all of 2026, and the newest is still August 6. No news outlet we can find has reported the ruling. We have not read Judge McCannon's order, so we cannot tell you whether he found the question neutral, found the $5 million estimate supportable, or ruled on something narrower, and we found no sign of an appeal. The case simply dropped off the council's closed-session agenda after August 24. The ballot is the city's, word for word.

Two other lines on the city's election pages deserve a slow read now that the litigation is over. The city's Measure BB page says, correctly, that “The City of Pacifica does not advocate for or against Measure BB.” A few lines later it says this:

“Measure BB includes strict accountability provisions, including public spending reports and annual independent financial audits.”

In August we read the ordinance. Chapter 3-20 has exactly one accountability section, 3-20.19, which subjects the tax to “the same independent annual audit requirements as other general fund revenue” and says the auditor's report “may be combined with the City's annual financial audit” — the one the city already does. There is no oversight committee, no sunset, and no restriction on use; Section 3-20.17 says the money is for “unrestricted general revenue purposes.” An annual audit is a real provision. “Strict” is an adjective, and it is the city's. The survey page, meanwhile, warns that without new revenue “core city services will need to be reduced… This could lead to fewer police officers and firefighters keeping our neighborhoods safe.” Pacifica is allowed to tell voters what happens if a tax fails. Readers can decide for themselves where informing ends.

On the form it checked “Not yet qualified” — meaning it had not yet raised or spent $2,000 — and through this morning it has filed no report of any money at all. City Hall

Price and Terms of Payment

The City Council has met behind closed doors three times in twenty-nine days to negotiate over the building it meets in. The agenda names a buyer's group. The city has said nothing else.

Item 00a on the closed-session agenda for September 28, verbatim:

“CONFERENCE WITH REAL PROPERTY NEGOTIATORS. Property: 2212 Beach Boulevard, Pacifica, California 94044. Agency negotiator: Sean Charpentier, City Manager and Michelle Marchetta Kenyon, City Attorney. Negotiating parties: Pravin Punjiya for Paramount Investor Group. Under negotiation: Price and terms of payment.”

2212 Beach Boulevard is the address printed at the top of every City Council agenda. It is the Council Chambers, on Beach Boulevard in Sharp Park, near the pier; public commenters describe the parcel as including the city's storage yard. The same item, with the same negotiating party, was on the closed-session agenda September 8 and again at a special meeting on Tuesday, October 6, at 5:30 p.m., called for that item alone; the agenda PDF for that one was created at 4:42 p.m. the day before. Three sessions in twenty-nine days. The September 8 video ends at the recess and the October 6 session was not recorded; the one report out on record, September 28, reads: “There is no reportable action for either close session.”

Everything else the public knows came from the public. At the September 28 meeting, Sam Casillas told the council it had “been negotiating the potential sale of these publicly owned council chambers and storage yard without the input of the community,” and objected to “giving it away to some outside hotel developer.” A second speaker, identified on the record only as Lyla, said she had “serious concerns about selling public land in an environmentally sensitive and increasingly vulnerable coastal area. That property is directly across from the pier.” The words “sale” and “hotel” are theirs. No city document we have found confirms either. The agenda's spelling of the negotiator's name is the only one we have.

The Brown Act lets a council close the door on price and terms when it is negotiating a property deal. It does not, by its terms, make a secret of the larger question — whether Pacifica should sell the room where Pacifica governs itself to a private investor. That question has not appeared on any open agenda. Neither has any explanation of why three meetings were needed. It is the city's own building; the agenda even tells you who is across the table. What it will not tell you is what the city wants for it.

One related detail, also from September. On September 8, in the same closed session, the council conferred about a second Beach Boulevard property — “Pacifica Pier-2100 Beach Boulevard,” negotiating party “Marlene Schroeder for State Lands Commission,” under negotiation “Price and terms of payment.” Six days later, on consent, the council rescinded the new State Lands lease for the pier it had approved in March, on the grounds of “the current closure of the pier, the need for significant additional emergency stabilization work, and the subsequent analysis of the options to bring the pier back to its original use.” The pier reverts to its 1971 permit. Two adjoining pieces of the Sharp Park waterfront, two rounds of private talks about money, one month.

The Coast

Closed Until Further Notice, or Open Since September 30

Two governments disagree about whether Rockaway Beach is closed for sewage. One of them runs the plant.

At 2:03 p.m. on Tuesday, September 29, San Mateo County Health posted a public notice. In full:

“Rockaway Beach and Calera Creek are closed due to a Sanitary Sewer Overflow occurrence and will remain closed until further notice.”

The county's own rules give that word a specific meaning: “When there is a confirmed sewage spill that directly affects a beach, the beach is posted as CLOSED.” It is a different and more serious category than the advisory signs that go up after a bad bacteria sample. As of this morning, two weeks later, the notice is still posted, and the county's website records it as never modified.

The City of Pacifica, which owns and operates the Calera Creek Water Recycling Plant on the creek in question, issued no press release. Its only public statement is a “For the Record” post dated Tuesday, October 6 — a week after the closure — and its account is very different:

“On Sunday evening on September 27th, the City of Pacifica observed an unusual operating condition… whereby the Plant automatically entered a rarely used operating mode designed to protect treatment. Staff initially suspected that incoming partially treated wastewater might have mixed with treated wastewater before it passed through filtration and disinfection and ultimately discharge.”

“Early news coverage described a 183,000-gallon sewage spill based on the initial State emergency report. That figure was a conservative estimate of potentially affected flow, not a confirmed volume…”

“On Wednesday, September 30th, the County Environmental Health Services authorized reopening Rockaway Beach.”

“Subsequent investigation and testing support the conclusion that the suspected treatment bypass did not occur… No sewage spilled outside the Plant's treatment system.”

So: the plant flipped into a protective mode overnight; staff reported a possible bypass to the state; the state figure was 183,000 gallons; signs went up Monday morning; samples that day came back under state thresholds; the county reopened the beach Wednesday; and the city now concludes there was no spill. That may all be true. But it means that for two weeks the county's public notice has told anyone who looked that Rockaway is closed, and the city has told anyone who found its “For the Record” page that it isn't. As of today, one of the two is wrong, and neither has corrected the other.

We could not find the “early news coverage” the city refers to, and we did not pull the state's spill report, which sits behind a search form. The most useful thing the city could do is easy: post the state report, the sample results and the county's reopening notice in one place. The most useful thing a surfer can do is call the county's beach posting line, (650) 599-1266, before paddling out at the creek mouth. Separately, the county's October 7 update, from samples taken October 5, has Linda Mar #5 posted with warning signs.

While We Were Out

Three Arcs Closed, One Seat Empty

Backdrop, August 22 through October 5, in order of how long Pacifica has been arguing about it.

The quarry, again. On September 28 the City Council unanimously denied Preserve@Pacifica's appeal and upheld the city's position that the 1,225-unit Coastal Crest project on the 86.39-acre Rockaway quarry needs a public vote under 1983's Ordinance 391-C.S. and must comply with the 2025 Land Use Plan. Councilmember Mayra Espinosa moved; Mary Bier seconded. (The Tribune printed the seconder as “Greg Bier,” a councilmember Pacifica does not have.) Developer attorney Alicia Guerra of Buchalter, per the meeting's automatic captions: “what we're going to end up with here is a mess,” a phrase she also used on August 3. Developer spokesman Joel Kamstra told the Tribune: “We are reviewing our options with counsel.” No suit is on file that we can find, and an unnamed “One Potential Case” anticipated-litigation item has stayed on the council's closed-session agenda meeting after meeting. Two loose ends from our own file are tied: the Planning Commission's August 3 denial was 5–0, with Commissioners Hauser and Ferguson absent, per its Resolution 2026-007, now in the council packet; and the “1996” date an AI-written account attached to a quarry vote is, per the city's staff report, the year the quarry's reclamation plan was approved. Mining stopped in 1987.

Short-term rentals: Pacifica swallowed it, and it's done. On August 25 the Planning Commission voted 6–0 to recommend accepting the Coastal Commission's modifications, after Commission staff reversed course at the last minute and said the city could apply them in the coastal zone only. On September 14 the council adopted Ordinance No. 907-C.S. 5–0: in the coastal zone, unhosted rentals may now run 120 nights a year and no primary-residence rule applies; inland, the old rules stand — 60 nights and primary residence. On Friday, October 9, the Coastal Commission's executive director reported the city's action “legally adequate to meet all of the Commission's conditional certification requirements,” and the item was recorded as “CONCURRED.” Certified, inside the October renewal window, on the schedule staff drew up in August. Councilmember Mayra Espinosa, to the Tribune: “We can have an ordinance, but if we don't enforce it, we don't have anything.”

A footnote on tone. On August 7, City Manager Sean Charpentier wrote the Coastal Commission that its reversal on rentals was “without precedent” and that if a public agency “did an about face like this, our constituencies would raise significant questions about our processes and our legitimacy.” On August 21 he wrote the federal government, which is reviewing California's coastal program at the Commerce Secretary's direction, praising the same Commission's “balanced and pragmatic approach to coastal protection,” which “is needed now more than ever.” The council ratified the second letter 5–0 on September 14. Both letters can be sincere. A city can be furious with a regulator and still prefer it to the alternative.

Alex Ferguson. Planning Commissioner Alex Ferguson, 37, died the night of August 29 in a canoeing accident on Tomales Bay, where he had gone with a friend to see the bioluminescence; per the Tribune, both men wore life jackets. He lived in Sharp Park with his wife and three young children and had served on the commission since 2020. His last recorded vote was an aye, on August 25, on the rental ordinance. The council adjourned in his memory, and on October 6 it held interviews to fill his seat.

And the other paper lost its reporter. Kathryn Wheeler, who covered Pacifica for the Tribune and Coastside News and whose reporting this paper has cited again and again, wrote her farewell on October 7: “This week is my last with Coastside News.” The paper has posted a listing for a staff writer. Until it fills it, a great deal of Pacifica's coverage will come from the paper's AI-generated “Coastie” feed, which this paper never cites alone. A newspaper founded on the disappearance of local news should say plainly that this is bad news.

Schools

Forty-Nine Point Three Percent

The county's annual letter on the Pacifica School District's finances is the best document of the fall, and it arrived as an information item, three weeks before ballots.

On September 4, Kevin J. Bultema, the San Mateo County Office of Education's deputy superintendent for business services, sent the Pacifica School District its annual fiscal report, copied to the State Superintendent and the State Controller. Its findings, verbatim:

“In May 2026, the Fiscal Crisis and Management Assistance Team (FCMAT) completed a Fiscal Health Risk Analysis… FCMAT concluded that the District's overall fiscal solvency risk level is high, with a score of 49.3%. In addition, specific conditions… most notably incomplete financial reporting, deficit spending without a governing board-approved corrective action plan, and weaknesses in internal controls – independently elevated Pacifica's fiscal risk.”

“The District did not meet the minimum reserve requirement of 3% in its 2026-27 Budget and the reserve levels dropped to negative numbers in the subsequent years.”

The letter does not lift the “lack of going concern” designation the county imposed in February; the district's own stabilization plan, updated September 30, still lists county oversight, an assigned fiscal expert and mandatory pre-review of labor contracts as “currently active.” Then came a lucky break. On July 20 the county told Pacifica that rising property taxes had flipped it to “community-funded” status for 2025–26, worth an extra $902,000 — one-time money, the district's consultant warned, that “Will need to be closely monitored.” The revised budget presented September 23 now shows positive reserves in all three years (the board's adoption vote a week later is not yet in published minutes). Its own slide lists what that picture leaves out: “Not Included: Certificated CY settlement; Mgmt + Conf settlements; Recent SpEd Settlements.” In other words, the teachers' raise, the administrators' raise and the special-education legal settlements.

The stabilization plan supplies the number we would most like a candidate to be asked about. Among the “unbudgeted liabilities” that brought on county oversight: “An unpaid unemployment bill for 2021 Q3 & Q4; 2022 all quarters; 2023 Q1 & Q2. This was an unbudgeted expenditure totalling $298,368.70.” Two years of unemployment-insurance bills to the state went unpaid, and surfaced only as an unbudgeted expense.

The plan also says what Measure T is actually for, in language the ballot doesn't use. The expiring parcel tax, Measure D, “is entirely responsible for sustaining five (5) Library Media Technicians, four (4) Guidance Counselors, and the comprehensive funding required for our district-wide Outdoor Education program.” If the renewal fails, those positions “will be fully eliminated” and families “will be required to pay out-of-pocket fees to participate in outdoor school.” Enrollment, counted September 18, is 2,473, down 286 students in four years. The plan separately schedules 13 full-time positions for elimination in 2027–28 regardless.

Two notes from the board's own published minutes. On August 19 the board, with only three of its four members present, “voted 3-0 to direct counsel to initiate litigation, with additional details to be disclosed when an action is filed.” We do not know against whom. And the superintendent's performance evaluation has been on the closed-session agenda at four consecutive meetings — August 19, September 9, September 23 and September 30 — with nothing reported out. Minutes for every September meeting are unpublished. The next board meeting is October 21.

Public Safety

Two Hundred and Six Stops, No Tows, Nine Days Left

The police log tells you where Pacifica's vehicle dwellers are being moved. The city's paperwork does not tell you where they are supposed to go.

Pacifica PD's daily bulletins record a call type called “Muni Code Violation – RV.” From August 21 through October 12 it appears 206 times. One hundred eighteen of those are on Esplanade Avenue. The disposition, 142 times, is “Vehicle Marked”; seven times it is “Citation,” once “Parking Cite.” Nothing was towed. This week brought 29 more: eight Esplanade stops in 42 minutes on Wednesday morning, October 7; a 2 a.m. pass on Friday, October 9; seven more on Esplanade between 9:46 and 10:23 a.m. Monday. Every one of the fifteen daytime Esplanade stops ended “Vehicle Marked.”

Linda Mar Boulevard has dropped out of the log entirely. It had ten RV calls between August 22 and September 10, and none since 5:15 p.m. on September 10. That is consistent with what the Pacifica Resource Center's representative told the council September 28, per the meeting's automatic captions: nine encampments in Pacifica, including the Linda Mar lot, “although nobody is actually there overnight,” and 84 people in all. The Pacific Beach Coalition's page for this Saturday's Esplanade cleanup describes that beach, unprompted, as having “magnificent cliffs, magical sunsets, frequent visits by ocean life, and overnight RV campers.”

Two deadlines bracket all of this. The Temporary Safe Parking Program in the Manor — the city's one program for people living in vehicles — “lapses at the end of this month,” a supporter told the council on September 14. No agenda since August has carried an item to extend it; the August check register shows no payment to the RV park that hosts it, after $51,000 in July. Whether it ended September 30 or was quietly extended, we could not confirm. And the Coastal Commission emergency permit under which Pacifica closed the Linda Mar lots overnight in July requires the city, by Thursday, October 22 — nine days from today — to remove the closure or file a complete regular permit application, with penalties of up to $11,250 a day. No such application appears on any Commission agenda or report through October. The city's July statement said it “will continue to work on a complete Coastal Development Permit for the nighttime closure.” That was eighty-one days ago.

Pier Watch

Closed 131 days · 123 days since the last pier press release · 117 days since the county asked the Governor for help · 3,917 days of continuous coastline emergency

The contract was ratified, and somebody asked the price. On August 24 the council approved the emergency agreement with Power Engineering Construction Co. unanimously, on consent. Before the vote, per the captions, a question from the dais: “what is the total cost ceiling on this agreement or or there is no cap at all?” Charpentier's answer was that the contract lets the city authorize work in phases, “as available funding from… either Caloes or the insurance company is available.” No dollar figure has appeared in any council document since.

Nothing has been built. On September 3 the city issued Power a “Limited Notice to Proceed” for investigation and design. The September 14 and 28 status reports both say: “Construction is not authorized under the current LNTP and will require separate authorization from the City,” and both say “The abutment and first deck of the Pier remain structurally unstable.” Then, on Friday, October 9, the pier page's first update in seven weeks: Power “will be installing culvert pipe and base rock as groundwork for a crane pad” at Beach Boulevard and Santa Rosa Avenue, with “crane mobilization” to follow “for the next few weeks.” Lower on the same page, under History, the city still describes its pier this way: “It is currently considered in good shape.”

More rock than reported. A new Coastal Commission emergency permit, G-2-26-0038, issued September 8, authorizes “approximately 300 rocks (each about 5x5 feet) or approximately 1,500 tons” of riprap moved from south of the pier to the base of the abutment, plus “a temporary porta potty.” The city's August 24 report described the same 300 boulders as about 900 tons. The permit expressly covers nothing else — “Any other development requires separate authorization” — and we found no state permit yet for Power's piles or for the abutment removal in its contract. By December 7 the city must pull the rock out or apply for a regular permit.

Sacramento declared an emergency, just not this one. On September 21 Governor Newsom proclaimed a statewide state of emergency for the developing El Niño, ordering the Coastal Commission to act on urgent emergency permits “within twenty-four hours of receipt.” The same day, a separate proclamation terminated the state emergencies for the December 2023–January 2024 and March 2024 storms in San Mateo County — the storms the city's own documents blame for the crack in the abutment. On October 9 the Governor's office celebrated a $2.125 million emergency repair of the Malibu Pier. The county's June 18 request that he proclaim an emergency for Pacifica is 117 days old. The county itself extended its local pier emergency a third time on October 6, Resolution No. 082155, moved by Supervisor Ray Mueller.

Washington. Rep. Sam Liccardo's newest press release, October 9, concerns whale alerts for West Coast mariners. His last about Pacifica was June 16. H.R. 9557 has seven cosponsors and has not moved.

What we couldn't confirm

What the judge actually decided. The city says Judge Mark McCannon ruled for it on September 3, “denying all relief to Petitioners.” We have not seen the order, its reasoning, or whether there will be an appeal. The court's public records index, which gave us the party list in August, now returns nothing for the case number, and its document portal is behind a reCAPTCHA we do not defeat.

What 2212 Beach Boulevard is for. We know the property, the negotiators and the topic. We do not know whether the city is selling, leasing or something else, what Paramount Investor Group proposes to build, or why three closed sessions were needed. “Hotel” is a public commenter's word.

Whether Rockaway is closed. The county says closed until further notice; the city says the county reopened it September 30. We did not reach either agency, and we did not pull the state spill report.

The Safe Parking Program and the Linda Mar permit. Whether the program ended September 30, and whether the city has filed the permit application due October 22. Neither appears in any city or Commission document we could find. The Commission's district office could answer the second in one phone call.

Whether the council met last night. Almost certainly not. Nobody said.
