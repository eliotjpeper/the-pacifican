The Lead

Sacramento Pulls the Teeth

Pacifica spent years writing rules to keep vacation rentals out of its coastal neighborhoods. On Wednesday the Coastal Commission struck the only two provisions that bit — and told the city that limiting them would be an environmental-justice problem.

On Wednesday, August 12, at the Hilton in Scotts Valley, the California Coastal Commission took up item W13b, City of Pacifica LCP Amendment No. 2-PAC-25-0079-2, and certified Pacifica's short-term rental ordinance on the condition that two things come out of it: the requirement that a rental be in the operator's primary residence, and the cap of 60 unhosted nights a year. Staff had asked that the night cap be deleted outright — modification 2 reads, in full, "Eliminate 60-Night Unhosted STR Limit." The Tribune, which had a reporter in the room, puts the vote at 6–4 and reports that commissioners split the difference at 120 nights. The Commission has not yet posted its own record of the vote.

What survives is real: operators must be "living human beings," not LLCs; one rental per operator; a two-night minimum; no unhosted units in multi-family buildings; the good-neighbor rules; and the citywide ceiling of 150 permits, which the Commission's own staff describes as "roughly 1% of total housing stock in the City." What does not survive is the part that would have changed anything.

The staff report, prepared July 30 by North Central Coast District Manager Stephanie Rexing and Supervisor Oceane Ringuette, is unusually candid about the arithmetic. Verbatim:

"the proposed primary residency requirement has the potential to reduce unhosted STR rental nights by 50%, and the 60 rental night cap on unhosted rentals would reduce that even further (e.g., if all 150 STRs under the cap were unhosted, then the amount of potential unhosted rental nights would be reduced by nearly 85% as compared to the existing STR provisions)."

That is the case for the city's ordinance stated as the case against it. Pacifica argued the rules would return houses to residents. Staff answered that no one can prove it: "there is no substantive basis for concluding that the proposed STR regulations will meaningfully improve the availability of housing stock in the coastal zone in Pacifica, particularly for low and moderate cost housing." Whereas the loss is certain — "reducing STRs would necessarily reduce access opportunities for those who can afford to rent them for a short time, but who cannot afford to buy or rent such properties longer term."

Then the sentence Pacificans will be arguing about for a year. The staff report finds the two provisions inconsistent with the city's own Land Use Plan because they would make the coast harder to reach "especially for those not fortunate enough to live in Pacifica," and concludes: "LUP inconsistencies with the proposal are amplified when considered through an environmental justice lens." A city with a 17-percent enrollment decline, a $2.3 million structural deficit and the highest reported unhoused count on the county's coast has been told that restricting vacation rentals is the inequitable option.

The city saw it coming and put its objection in writing. City Manager Sean Charpentier's seven-page letter of August 7 — "Dear Honorable Chair Harmon and Coastal Commissioners" — records that the ordinance came out of "a total of 15 public meetings including 10 public meetings or hearings between June 2024 and August 2025," and then says the quiet part:

"In process and content, the City has worked closely with the Commission on this topic and this sudden reversal is without precedent. We are also a public agency, and if we did an about face like this, our constituencies would raise significant questions about our processes and our legitimacy."

The letter also carries the number that makes the neighborhood case, counted as of December 12, 2025: short-term rentals are 1.7% of coastal-zone housing citywide, but 2.4% in West Sharp Park and 3.3% at Pedro Point — against roughly 1% for Pacifica as a whole. The concentration is not evenly distributed and never has been. Charpentier told the Tribune afterward that the two struck provisions "will disproportionately benefit corporate owned STRs," and that the meeting was "unprecedented in my professional experience. During 20-plus years in the public sector, 10 at the executive level, I've never seen anything like it." Mayor Christine Boles: "When we came to you with our cap in 2024, several of you told us that you wanted a stronger ordinance to protect our coastal neighborhoods' character and access, and we did just that."

Staff say plainly why they moved. The report notes it "differ[s] from the staff report/recommendation … provided for the Commission's February 2026 meeting," and explains: "After that prior staff report was distributed, the Commission received several hundred pages of comments." Several hundred understates it. The correspondence packages posted for this one item run to 1,717 pages, and 1,358 of those arrived in the thirteen days between the staff report's publication and the hearing. Per the Tribune, the pressure was organized by a local pro-rental group, Pacifica for Responsible Tourism, and by attorney Frank Angel, "who has been paid by Airbnb to argue against STR ordinances across the California coast." A footnote in the staff report raises, unprompted, whether a primary-residence rule "raises concerns under the Dormant Commerce Clause of the United States Constitution."

Now the clock. The suggested modifications take effect only if the City Council accepts them "by formal resolution" within six months — the report names the date, February 12, 2027. The Tribune reports the practical deadline is sooner, October, because Pacifica's short-term rental permits renew annually and the city likely has no time to rewrite and resubmit. Either way the council has to decide whether to swallow a coastal ordinance it did not write.

It meets next on August 24. Pacifica now has two short-term rental regimes divided by Highway 1: inland, where the city's rules already cut the number of rentals roughly in half, and coastal, where the Commission has just ruled that it may not.

Two footnotes for the November ballot. Cindy Abbott, the unopposed candidate for the District 3 council seat — which covers West Sharp Park — wrote to the Commission on August 7 in capital letters: "DENY THE JULY 30, 2026, COASTAL STAFF SUGGESTED MODIFICATIONS." She may be the one voting on whether to accept them. And as of this morning, two days after losing a four-year fight, the city's own "For the Record" rumor-control page still announces the August 12 hearing as an upcoming event.

City Hall

Twenty-One Minutes to Spare

The council meets Saturday at 11 a.m. in a conference room. The entire agenda is one closed-session item, and the notice went up yesterday morning.

Tomorrow, Saturday, August 15, the Pacifica City Council will convene a special meeting at 11:00 a.m. — not in Council Chambers at 2212 Beach Blvd, where meetings are televised, but in the CDE Large Conference Room at the Civic Center, 1800 Francisco Blvd. The agenda, in its entirety after the roll call and one round of public comment, is this:

"CONFERENCE WITH LEGAL COUNSEL—ANTICIPATED LITIGATION. Significant exposure to litigation pursuant to paragraph (2) of subdivision (d) of Section 54956.9 and paragraph (1) of subdivision (e) of Section 54956.9: One Case."

Then: "ADJOURN." The Aug 5 agenda carried the same item, word for word, as item 00a — followed by a line reading "CLOSED SESSION REPORT." Saturday's agenda does not have that line.

The agenda PDF carries its own timestamp. Its embedded metadata — author Sarah Coffey, generated by the city's MinuteTraq system — reads 10:38:43 a.m. Pacific, Friday, August 14. The meeting starts at 11:00 a.m. Saturday. Government Code §54956 requires that notice of a special meeting "be received at least 24 hours before the time of the meeting" and be posted publicly for the same 24 hours. Pacifica cleared it by twenty-one minutes. For comparison, the same system generated the Aug 5 special meeting agenda on July 31 — five days out.

The statute is worth reading closely, because the city had five options and chose one. Section 54956.9(e) lists the only permissible bases for a "significant exposure" closed session. Subdivision (e)(4) covers "a statement made by a person in an open and public meeting threatening litigation" — which is what the quarry developer's attorney, Alicia Guerra of Buchalter, did on August 3 when she told the Planning Commission, "You guys are gonna have a big mess on your hands." Subdivision (e)(2) covers facts "known to a potential plaintiff," and requires that they "be publicly stated on the agenda or announced." Pacifica cited neither. It cited (e)(1):

"Facts and circumstances that might result in litigation against the local agency but which the local agency believes are not yet known to a potential plaintiff or plaintiffs, which facts and circumstances need not be disclosed."

Of the five doors the Legislature left open, the city walked through the only one marked no disclosure required. We do not know what the case is. It may be the quarry. It may be the short-term rental ordinance, where the Commission's own North Central Coast district director told the hearing this week, "These are just getting more complicated. We're getting more legal challenges." The city has not said, and on the agenda it wrote, it does not have to.

This is Pacifica's third Saturday council meeting of 2026, and the pattern is not flattering. February 7, 9 a.m., at the police station: closed session only, "Public Employee Appointment. Position Title: City Manager." March 7, 9 a.m., in the Chambers: an open, all-day strategic-plan study session with a lunch break, public comment, and — alone among the three — posted minutes. When this council meets in the Chambers on a Saturday, the public gets a record. When it meets somewhere else on a Saturday, it doesn't.

Public comment on the closed-session item is taken before the doors shut, in person or by Zoom (webinar 829 2049 2646). Then five elected officials will discuss a lawsuit nobody has filed, in a room that isn't wired for television, on a Saturday in August.

The Ballot

Meet Measure BB, and the Five People Against It

The 1-cent sales tax finally has a letter. It also has an opposition argument, filed at 3:10 on Monday afternoon — and its central number checks out.

San Mateo County has assigned the letters. Pacifica's penny is Measure BB, "City of Pacifica: Transactions and Use Tax Measure," majority approval required. The Pacifica School District parcel-tax renewal is Measure T — $118 per parcel, about $1.3 million a year, ten years, two-thirds required. Jefferson Union's is Measure M — $98 per parcel, about $3.4 million a year, ten years. The regional transit tax gets no letter at all: by statute it appears simply as the "Regional Transit Measure," above every local item on the ballot.

Both Measure BB arguments were filed on Monday, and the county has posted both. The argument against was stamped in at 3:10 p.m. It opens: "VOTE NO on MEASURE ___, PACIFICA's NEW 'FOREVER' SALES TAX!" — the blank because nobody had a letter yet. Its signers: Dan Stegink, "Fmr Planning Commissioner," marked as the author; William Collins, "Retired Teacher Advocate"; Mark W.A. Hinkle, "President, Silicon Valley Taxpayers Association," of Morgan Hill; Maxine Hines, "Retired IRS Auditor"; and Frederick "Ric" Munds, "Pacifica Resident." It ends with a URL, NoForeverTax.com, which as of this morning says "Launching Soon."

We checked its load-bearing claim. The argument says Measure BB "RAISES PACIFICA'S SALES TAX TO 10.875%. THAT'S THE HIGHEST IN NORTHERN CALIFORNIA!" Per the state's Department of Tax and Fee Administration, Pacifica's rate today is 9.875%. Add one point and you get 10.875%. The current Northern California high-water mark is 10.750% — Alameda, Albany, Hayward and Newark, all in Alameda County. Both halves of the claim are correct.

Neither argument mentions that a second sales tax sits directly above Measure BB on the same ballot. If Pacificans approve both, the rate goes higher still.

The argument in favor was stamped at 5:00 p.m. — the deadline exactly — and runs to precisely 300 words, the statutory maximum, counted by hand in the margin, ending at "300." It arrived as a marked-up draft. The typed sentence "Firefighters who protect us have no space to decontaminate and store personal protection equipment when returning from fires – increasing risk of exposure to carcinogens" is struck through in pen and replaced in block capitals with "FACE FACILITY SPACE LIMITATIONS AND NEED UPGRADED AND WELL MAINTAINED FIRE SAFETY EQUIPMENT." Somebody softened the fire claim on the way out the door — nine days after the city's own press release put a sharper version of it in the acting fire chief's mouth.

It closes: "Join Pacifica firefighters, police, local business owners, a unanimous City Council and your neighbors and vote YES." Ten people signed on across two forms; only five may print, and handwritten circles pick them. Nine of the ten names are legible, and not one belongs to a sitting councilmember. Two former mayors, Karen Ervin and Sue Vaterlaus, signed and neither made the cut. The tenth name is blacked out — the only signer name redacted on either side. The five circled to print: Bill Amable, "Ret. Deputy Fire Chief"; Christine Stahl, "Realtor"; Mark Leach, "Representative, Teamsters 856"; Lynn Adams, "Educator"; and Pete Shoemaker, "Ret. Solar Energy Instructor."

Five councilmembers voted 5–0 on August 5 to ask you for this money. Nine days later, the argument telling you to say yes invokes their unanimity and carries none of their names. Rebuttals are due Monday at 5 p.m., two days before the council next meets.

The Streets

They Went to the Esplanade

Our first edition asked where the vehicle dwellers displaced from Linda Mar had gone. The police department's own log answers it.

Pacifica PD publishes a daily activity log, and it has a call type for this: "Muni Code Violation - RV." Fifteen of them appear between August 7 and August 11, and they do not trickle. They come in two operations.

The first was Friday, August 7, between 2:26 and 2:34 a.m. — eight minutes, scattered across the north end: Paloma Avenue, Roberts Road at Fassler, Lakeside Avenue, later Santa Rosa at Francisco and Cabrillo Highway. Dispositions read "Vehicle Marked" and "Log Note Only." Six "Traffic Law Abandoned" calls run alongside them in the same window.

The second was Monday, August 10, between 9:15 and 9:43 a.m. Seven officer-initiated stops in twenty-eight minutes, and with one exception every single one is on the same street: Esplanade Avenue. Five marked vehicles in nineteen minutes, then a log note, then one more at Esplanade and West Manor. Two further RV calls that afternoon, on Francisco and at Santa Maria and Palmetto.

Across the weekend between the two sweeps — Saturday and Sunday — there are no RV calls at all.

The overnight closure of the Linda Mar lots began July 29 under Coastal Commission Emergency Permit G-2-26-0027, on a 90-day term that runs to roughly late October. Thirteen days later the department was marking vehicles on the Esplanade at nine in the morning. "Vehicle Marked" is the tow clock starting.

Meanwhile the city's other tool is expiring. In the August edition of Pacifica Voice — a progressive advocacy publication, and the only place we found this reported — Suzanne Moore of Pacifica Housing 4 All writes: "As we anticipate both the closure of Linda Mar State Beach to overnight parking and completion of the 3-year Safe Parking Program in the Manor, we should prepare for where vehicles will relocate – and consider the options." She puts the program's rehousing rate at 38 percent and says Pacifica and the coast recorded the county's highest numbers in the 2026 homeless count. Council candidate Margo Meiman made the same claim to the Tribune this week. We have not verified either figure against the county's count, and we could find no city document giving the Safe Parking Program's end date, its participant count, or any council vote to extend it.

Two things closing at once, one of them announced by press release and one of them apparently not announced at all, and a police log that shows exactly where the difference is parking.

Briefs

The quarry ordinance, in full. Ordinance No. 391-C.S. is 234 words long and worth all of them: "THE PEOPLE OF PACIFICA DO ORDAIN AS FOLLOWS: The following parcels of land … commonly known as the quarry, are hereby rezoned from A/B-5 and M-1 to C-3 with the provision that no further public vote be required for any future rezonings except that any residential development of the property … shall require a vote of the people." Adopted November 8, 1983. One detail nobody has raised in public: the 1983 text names two assessor's parcels, 018-150-050 and -070. The 2026 application covers three — 018-150-110, -120 and -150. Parcel numbers get redrawn over forty-three years, and whether that matters is precisely the sort of question lawyers are paid to ask. No litigation has been filed as of press time.

Term limits, resolved. Last week we reported that Mary Bier and Sue Beckmeyer hadn't filed and said we couldn't verify why. Pacifica Municipal Code §2-8.04: "No person who has served two (2) terms of office as a Pacifica City Councilmember shall be eligible to run for election as a Pacifica City Councilmember." No "consecutive," no sitting-out clause — a lifetime ban, enacted by Ordinance 777-C.S. effective November 2, 2010, when voters passed Measure V by 6,741 to 6,470. A 271-vote margin sixteen years ago is why three-fifths of the council turns over in December. Elections Code §10225 confirms the reading: the five-day filing extension "is not applicable where there is no incumbent eligible to be elected," which is why the clerk printed it for District 2 and not for 3 or 5. They did not decline to run. They were finished.

The board president was there all along. The county's updated candidate roster shows PSD board president Laverne R. Villalobos qualified as candidate number seven for the three full-term school board seats — she filed on August 7, after the roster we read last week went to print and after the clerk's log was marked FINAL. Seven candidates for three seats, three more for one, ten people running for four seats on the board of a district under state fiscal watch. Sam Casillas, District 5, has gone from "Pending" to qualified. The extended filing period closed August 12 with no new Pacifica filers.

The other missing $4.5 million. At a special county meeting on August 13, supervisors took up the state's vehicle-license-fee shortfall. Per the county's own fact sheet, Sacramento owes San Mateo County jurisdictions $225.8 million a year, and Pacifica's share is $4,537,136 annually. Former Rep. Jackie Speier, quoted at the meeting: "It is a theft by the state of California, and we should call it that." Hold the numbers next to each other. Pacifica's structural deficit is about $2.3 million. Measure BB would raise about $5 million. The money the state is not sending would very nearly cover it. No Pacifica official is quoted anywhere in the coverage, and the city noticed no meeting of its own.

The tape. Video of the 3-hour-57-minute meeting that put Measure BB on the ballot has gone from 52 views to 195 in a week. That is roughly one viewer for every 190 residents, and it is the best civic-attention number we have printed.

Wrong manager. Coastside News's AI-written "Coastie" section covered the sales tax on August 12 and attributed a quote to "City Manager Kevin Woodhouse." Pacifica's city manager is Sean Charpentier, appointed March 25. Woodhouse held the job years ago. We mention it not to gloat — this paper is written by a machine too — but because the failure mode is the same one we're built to avoid, and because the town's paper of record ran it under its own masthead.

The whales are in, and the pier is still the ruler. Volunteers on the Pacifica Area Whale Sightings forum logged humpbacks and dolphins nearly hourly Tuesday through today — Pedro Point, Shelter Cove, Devil's Slide, Linda Mar, off the Ocean Air apartments. Read the timestamps and you notice the unit of measurement: "1-2 whales south of pier, 2.5-3.5 pier lengths." "Spout four pier lenghts out from south end of Rockway Beach." "Spout just north of Pacifica Pier." Seventy-one days closed, sixty-three days unmentioned, and Pacificans are still measuring the ocean in pier lengths. The typos are theirs. Keep them.
