Corrections

We have printed Measure T, the Pacifica School District's parcel-tax renewal, as $118 per parcel for ten years, and calculated from that a combined PSD parcel burden of $216. The county counsel's impartial analysis says the amount is “subject to annual adjustment for inflation based on the ‘Consumer Price Index for all Urban Consumers San Francisco Area.’” It is $118 in the first year, July 1, 2027, rising each year through June 30, 2037. Ten years will cost more than ten times $118. The opposition argument makes the same error in the other direction, putting the ten-year total at “$1,180.”

The Lead

Phase 3 Is Called “Abutment Removal”

The city has signed an emergency construction contract for the pier. The agreement carries no price, it drew one bidder, and it contemplates taking part of the structure out. None of this was announced. It surfaced because somebody read page 203 of a 388-page PDF posted at 11:17 on a Wednesday night.

On Monday, August 24, the Pacifica City Council will be asked to approve, on its consent calendar — the part of the agenda reserved for items nobody expects to discuss — a resolution ratifying an Emergency Public Works Agreement that City Manager Sean Charpentier has already signed. The contractor is Power Engineering Construction Co. of Alameda. The subject is the Pacifica Municipal Pier, closed since June 4.

The staff report tells the council what the work will cost in these words, and only these: “The ultimate cost of the emergency work will depend on the results of ongoing technical investigations, field conditions, regulatory requirements, construction sequencing, and the scope of stabilization measures determined to be necessary as the work progresses.” The FISCAL IMPACT section of a multimillion-dollar contract contains no dollar sign.

Page 203 of the same packet does. Under the heading SECTION 07 COST PROPOSAL, dated July 29, 2026, Power's own itemized budget reads:

Total Phase 1 Budget: $4,294,064 — 114 working days.
Total Phase 2 Budget: $2,426,492 — 77 working days.
Alternate Phase 1A Shoring: $3,030,420.

Project assumption number 5, verbatim: “No contingency is included in this budget.”

Now the part that matters more than the money.

Section 3.4 of the agreement lists the anticipated phases. Phase 2 shores the pier's first span. Phase 1 and 1A protect Beach Boulevard and its utilities. Then:

“Phase 3 – Abutment Removal. Phase 3 may include removal of the deteriorated pier abutment and associated sheet pile wall, together with related drainage improvements, once the abutment is no longer required for structural support. Phase 3 removal shall not be performed until it is reasonably clear, based on engineering findings, that demolition of the abutment will not destabilize the Pacifica Pier, Beach Boulevard, adjacent utilities, or the shoreline protection system.”

“Phase 4 – Shoreline Protection. Phase 4 may include placement of supplemental shoreline protection (riprap) as necessary, following removal of the deteriorated abutment under Phase 3…”

Read the sequence plainly. Drive two 36-inch steel pipe piles into the sand just west of where the pier meets land and jack the first span up onto them. Build a wall of three-foot-diameter king piles to hold Beach Boulevard. Then demolish the abutment — the concrete-and-sheet-pile block that connects the pier to Pacifica — and put rock where it used to be.

The staff report says the same thing in a subordinate clause on page three: the scope will depend on field conditions, “including the extent to which portions of the Pier and abutment are ultimately repaired, further stabilized, or removed.” And the agreement, describing what happens after the emergency work ends, refers without comment to “the Pier and former abutment area” and to a future long-term plan “including rehabilitation, replacement, or removal.”

This is the first time the City of Pacifica has put demolition of any part of the pier on paper. It did so on a consent calendar.

The procurement matters. Under Public Contract Code sections 20168 and 22050 the city skipped competitive bidding: on July 14 it sent an emergency RFP to three marine construction firms, and on July 29 it got one proposal — from Power, which repaired this pier's underside in 2002 for $500,000 and has brought along Scott Holmes, PE, the former Pacifica City Engineer, as a consultant. Power is blunt about what it found: “Currently the landside abutment of the first pier span is settling and rotating… A failure of this pier span could be detrimental to the stability of the remaining pier bents.” And on the arithmetic: “Financially the pier is a very large asset in a relatively small city — which we anticipate may strain the repair budget.” It expects “a strong El Nino winter.”

Which brings up the calendar, and the calendar is the accountability story. The packet contains two versions of Power's schedule. In the first, notice of intent to award takes five days and Phase 1 construction runs October 1 to November 30. In the second, notice of intent to award takes eighteen days — ending Friday, August 21, today — notice to proceed slips to August 28, and Phase 1 construction now runs October 20 to January 13, 2027. The staff report's stated purpose is “the need to advance critical work ahead of the winter storm season.” The revised schedule finishes the first phase six weeks deeper into it.

Three dates for the pedantic, all from the city's own documents. The agenda packet's PDF metadata stamps its creation at 11:17 p.m. Pacific on Wednesday, August 19. The staff report describes an agreement “dated August 20, 2026” and already executed. The agreement's own first line says it is “entered into as of August 25, 2026” — the day after the council votes to ratify it. Monday's resolution requires a four-fifths vote and rests, per the agreement's recitals, on findings “set forth in the minutes of its meeting.” The newest approved City Council minutes posted to the city's website are from April 27.

The city issued no press release about any of this. It has issued six press releases in all of 2026; the most recent is August 6, about the sales tax, and it does not mention the pier. The last time Pacifica said anything publicly about its pier was June 12 — seventy days ago. In the intervening ten weeks the city has placed nine hundred tons of rock around the abutment, run an emergency procurement, received a single bid, negotiated a contract with an insurer's sign-off, and put demolition on the table.

Residents will find out on Monday, if they read to item five. Or they can read the fine print of Public Contract Code section 22050, which requires the council to “review the emergency action at each regularly scheduled meeting until the action is terminated.” The pier is now a standing agenda item. It has been a standing local emergency since January 22, 2016 — 3,864 days, renewed every thirty.

The Ballot

Stegink v. Coffey

The two lead signers of the argument against Measure BB sued the city clerk and the county elections officer on August 17. Nobody has reported it. Meanwhile the voter pamphlet now carries two rebuttals that flatly contradict each other about whether the tax has any oversight. We read the ordinance.

Item 00b of Monday's closed session, verbatim from the city's own agenda:

“CONFERENCE WITH LEGAL COUNSEL – EXISTING LITIGATION… Name of case: Dan Stegink, et al. v. Sarah Coffey, et al. San Mateo County Superior Court Case No. 26-CIV06367.”

The Superior Court's public records index returns seven parties, all filed August 17, 2026:

Plaintiffs: Dan Stegink, William Collins.
Defendants: Sarah Coffey, Mark Church.
Other: Michelle Marchetta Kenyon, City of Pacifica, City Council of City of Pacifica.

Stegink and Collins are the first and second signers of the ballot argument against Measure BB. Coffey is the Pacifica city clerk. Church is San Mateo County's chief elections officer. Kenyon is the Pacifica city attorney — and the author of the impartial analysis of Measure BB that voters will read in their pamphlet, signed “/s/ Michelle Marchetta Kenyon, City Attorney.”

We have not read the petition; the court's document portal is behind a reCAPTCHA and we do not defeat those. But the party list is close to self-explanatory. Elections Code section 9295 gives any voter ten calendar days after the ballot-material filing deadline to “seek a writ of mandate or an injunction requiring any or all of the materials to be amended or deleted,” and specifies the caption: “The elections official shall be named as respondent, and the person or official who authored the material in question shall be named as real parties in interest.” A writ issues “only upon clear and convincing proof that the material in question is false, misleading, or inconsistent with the requirements of this chapter.” Arguments were due August 10; the ten days ran out August 20. The people who authored the argument in favor of Measure BB are not parties. The city attorney and the city council are.

Stegink surfaced the case himself, at 7:31 p.m. on August 17, in the comment thread beneath the Tribune's story on the tax: “San Mateo County Superior Court case: #26CIV06367 will also have something to do with it.” He filed his side's rebuttal argument at the clerk's counter at 3:10 that same afternoon.

What the rebuttals say, and what the ordinance says

Both Measure BB rebuttals were stamped by the Pacifica city clerk on August 17 — which settles a discrepancy we printed twice and could not resolve. The city said the seventeenth and the county's calendar said the eighteenth, and both were right: city measures are filed with the city clerk, school-district measures with the county. Measure T's and Measure M's rebuttals carry an August 18 county stamp. Ours is not a contradiction; it is two counters.

The no side filed at 3:10 p.m., 238 words, same five signers as before, hand-counted in the margin:

“Measure BB raises Pacifica's sales tax to 10.875% — the highest in Northern California! Every gallon of gas will cost another nickel… City Hall says it's broke — but it just built a $12,000,000 Civic Center and pays 28 employees over $250,000 each in pay/benefits… There's no ‘oversight’. The word doesn't appear anywhere in the ordinance.”

The yes side filed fifty minutes later, at 4:00 p.m., 237 words, and opened by attacking a zip code:

“What does an organization led by a Morgan Hill resident know about Pacifica? Nothing… Every dime of Measure BB STAYS RIGHT HERE IN PACIFICA… Measure BB requires public spending reports, community oversight and annual independent financial audits to ensure accountability.”

Neither side could see the other's filing. Voters will see both on the same page. So we read the ordinance, which is attached to Resolution No. 61-2026 and posted by the county as a scan. Chapter 3-20 of the Pacifica Municipal Code runs from Section 3-20.01 to Section 3-20.19, and 3-20.19 is the last one. In full:

“Section 3-20.19. – Annual Audit. The proceeds resulting from this transactions and use tax shall subject to the same independent annual audit requirements as other general fund revenue. The independent auditor's report, which shall include an accounting of the revenues received and expenditures made from the transactions and use tax, will be presented annually to the City Council and made available for public review. Further, such independent auditor's report and such presentation thereof to the City Council may be combined with the City's annual financial audit.”

(The missing word in “shall subject to” is in the enacted text.)

Verdict, three ways. “Annual independent financial audits” — true, though the ordinance expressly permits folding the report into the audit the city already does. “Public spending reports” — defensible; the auditor's report must include an accounting of the tax's revenues and expenditures and be made available for public review. “Community oversight” — no. There is no oversight committee in the ordinance, and the opponents are right that the word does not appear in it. Two other sections say exactly what the opponents claim: 3-20.16, “The tax imposed by this chapter shall remain in effect until repealed by the City's voters,” and 3-20.17, “The proceeds… shall be for unrestricted general revenue purposes of the City.”

Since we were checking, we checked the opposition's other numbers against primary sources. The $69,675,036 net pension liability and $65,707,038 long-term debt are exact lines from the city's audited FY2025 financial statements — with a caveat the opponents omit: $45,758,285 of that debt, seventy percent, sits in the sewer enterprise and is repaid from sewer rates, not the general fund Measure BB would feed. The 28 employees over $250,000 in pay and benefits is exactly right for 2025 per the State Controller's payroll export, up from 24 in 2024; only eight clear $250,000 in wages alone. And the best-paid person in Pacifica city government is not the city manager but a fire captain, at $457,312 all in, of which $217,164 was overtime against $155,580 in regular pay — a city staffed about twenty percent below its neighbors, paying for the gap in overtime. “America's worst small city to start a business” is a real WalletHub finding from April: 1,334th of 1,334. The weak claim is the “$12,000,000 Civic Center”; the city borrowed $4 million for it, in 2021, at 1.97 percent.

One last number, and it is the strangest in this election. Across both the county's and the city's campaign-finance systems for all of 2026, there is no Measure BB committee at all, for or against; NoForeverTax.com, printed in a ballot argument, has none behind it and still says “Launching Soon.” Of the five people running for city council, only Mayor Christine Boles has a committee, and her January–June filing reports $0.00 raised and $222.95 spent — itemized, in its entirety, as a post office box, stamps and cards from the Pacifica USPS.

The Coast

Forty-One Houses

The Coastal Commission's vote is now confirmed: 6–4, 120 nights. So is the thing Pacifica has spent nine days not saying out loud — that it has no choice but to accept.

Last week we printed the 6–4 tally and the 120-night cap as the Tribune's reporting, single-sourced, and declined to call them fact. They are fact. The Commission's own agenda system records item W13b's outcome as “APPROVED WITH MODIFICATIONS,” and the captioned record of the August 12 hearing carries four roll calls on the item, which ran four hours and four minutes.

The sequence: reject Pacifica's ordinance as submitted, 10–0. Grandfather existing rentals, 1–9, failed. Cap unhosted stays at 180 nights, 5–5, failed. Cap them at 120 — moved by Chair Meagan Harmon — 6–4. Certify as modified, 6–4. Voting yes: Kalmick, Notthoff, Wilson, Jackson, Kelley, Harmon. Voting no: Lopez, Preciado, O'Malley, and Vice Chair Caryl Hart.

Do not mistake those four for Pacifica's friends. Hart had moved to certify with no night cap at all, per staff; the body amended 120 nights onto her motion over her objection, and she then voted against her own motion. The minority wanted the city to get less, not more. Hart, on the record: “what is the role of the Coastal Commission? We're not the housing commission, we're the coastal commission.” Harmon closed the item at the gavel: “That was messy, but this is how the policy gets made.”

Now the number that reframes everything. The staff addendum, generated at 8:30 p.m. the night before the hearing, records in a footnote that Pacifica's rules have already cut short-term rentals from 145 to 75 citywide, and that of those, 41 are in the coastal zone — “0.025% of the City's overall housing stock.” Two years of hearings, 1,717 pages of correspondence, an Airbnb-funded attorney, ex parte contacts, a seven-page protest from the city manager and a four-hour state hearing, over forty-one houses.

The city said nothing publicly for nine days. Its “For the Record” page, dated August 7, still bills the August 12 hearing in the future tense; last Friday that page was two days stale, today it is nine. There was no press release and no item on Monday's agenda.

Then, at 9:44 a.m. this morning, the city posted a staff report for a joint City Council–Planning Commission study session on Tuesday, August 25, followed by a Planning Commission special meeting at 7. It confirms the modifications — “Increase the maximum number of rental nights for unhosted STRs from 60 to 120 nights” — and then concedes the position:

“The only option for applying the current STR Ordinance to areas within the Coastal Zone for the coming year (November 2026–October 2027) is to accept, agree, and adopt the modifications.”

The schedule staff propose: study session Tuesday, council second reading September 14, submission to the Executive Director September 15, the Commission's October hearing on the 7th through the 9th, effective October 15 — two weeks before the annual permit renewal window closes on October 31. One line reveals how tight that is: “Staff have requested confirmation that the modifications could be brought to the October Coastal Commission hearing and are waiting for a response.” The formal deadline is February 12, 2027. The real one is a phone call Pacifica is waiting on.

Public Safety

Five Hundred Feet Across the Street

Pacifica police logged thirty-five RV enforcement stops in nine days, nineteen of them on Esplanade Avenue, and issued the first citations of the campaign. Meanwhile, at a state hearing nobody in Pacifica watched, two residents answered the question this paper has been asking since its first edition.

The department's daily activity log carries a call type reading “Muni Code Violation – RV.” Between August 12 and August 20 it appears thirty-five times. Nineteen of those are on Esplanade Avenue, the bluff-top street in the Manor whose 300 and 400 blocks the city's own engineering reports describe as eroding toward public utilities.

The operations are officer-initiated, weekday, and in daylight. On Friday, August 14, ten Esplanade-corridor stops ran from 8:30 to 9:48 a.m. Three of them — at 8:44, 9:01 and 9:10 — carry the disposition “Citation.” That is the first escalation past “Vehicle Marked” in this series; all seven of the August 10 stops produced markings only. Monday, August 17 brought five more stops in seventeen minutes. Wednesday brought two. By Thursday the Esplanade calls had stopped and the day's remaining RV calls, scattered across Francisco, San Pedro and San Jose, came back “Gone On Arrival.”

Vol. 1, No. 1 asked where the people displaced by the July 29 overnight closure of the Linda Mar lots had gone. On August 12, during general public comment at the Coastal Commission — hours before the same body took up Pacifica's rental ordinance — a Pacifica speaker answered:

“I was counting between 17 and 21 vehicles every morning… Well, they've all moved, 500 feet across the street into the county's leased SamTrans lot that they lease from Caltrans. Again, there's no services, no running water, no showers or anything… The city and the county cannot simply ignore them and put them in a neighborhood and offer no humanitarian services at all.”

A second speaker identified herself as representing “the newly formed San Mateo County Homeless Union, Pacifica branch” — an organization that appears nowhere in any Pacifica record we have — and said this:

“The RV dwellers have been subjected to repeated vandalism… They've been shot with paintballs, BB guns, and even in one instance a vehicle dweller was shot in the back with BB guns. They found the youth that did this, the juveniles, and the vehicle dweller was so compassionate he didn't want to ruin a young person's life.”

Set that against the city's own paperwork. The emergency permit Pacifica obtained to close the lots — officially “Pacifica State Beach Temporary Overnight Parking Lot Closure,” applied for July 22 and issued July 24, a two-day turnaround — justifies the closure by citing “persistent vandalism, illegal dumping, extended vehicle occupancy, damage to public facilities, sanitation hazards, and unexpected conflict between users.” A witness has now told the Commission, on its own record, that some of the vandalism ran the other way.

The permit's conditions are worth the city's attention. Condition 3 requires the temporary work finished within thirty days of issuance — “i.e., by Saturday, August 23, 2026,” the day after tomorrow. Condition 8 gives Pacifica until October 22, sixty-two days, to remove everything or file a complete regular coastal development permit application. Condition 9 is stiffer than we reported last week: administrative penalties “of up to $11,250 per day per violation” and a civil action carrying “daily penalties of up to $15,000 per violation per day.” No regular application exists; the Commission's future-agenda page lists a North Central Coast heading with nothing under it. The Commission spent ninety seconds on the permit in August. District Director Dan Carl: “Emergency permit is simply being reported and doesn't require commission concurrence.”

And here is the context Pacifica has been arguing about without evidence. San Mateo County's 2026 one-day homeless count, taken January 29 and published by the county Human Services Agency, puts Pacifica's unsheltered population at 228 — the highest of any city in the county, ahead of Redwood City at 175 and Daly City at 147. Pacifica alone is 19.9 percent of the county's 1,145 unsheltered people; add the unincorporated coastside and Half Moon Bay and the coast is 29.6 percent. Countywide, 69 percent of unsheltered people live in a vehicle — 42 percent in a car or van, 27 percent in an RV.

Pacifica Voice and council candidate Margo Meiman have both been saying Pacifica leads the county. We carried it as unverified for two editions. It is verified. Three caveats belong with it: the figure is unsheltered-only and Pacifica has no shelter; the county's own note says vehicle counts “use an occupancy formula rather than a direct count”; and the full report is not out until fall.

As for the city's own answer to vehicle dwelling — the Temporary Safe Parking Program — it has left almost no documentary trace. No staff report, no council item, no page giving an end date or a participant count. There is, in the July check register attached to Monday's agenda, one line: check 8409, July 2, 2026, MHC San Francisco RV Resort, $51,000.00, the RV park on Palmetto Avenue in the Manor. It is the first primary document tying city money to the program, and it says nothing else.

What the record does say comes from the May 11 minutes, published for the first time in Monday's packet, three and a half months late. Mayor Christine Boles, in the strategic-plan session:

“the issue is, in terms of budget, it is a big difference in working toward solutions or continuing a program we have that is expensive. She stated that we will put a pin in that one.”

Five residents raised the program in public comment at the May 26 meeting alone; four, per the minutes, “emphasized continuing the Temporary Safe Parking Program in light of its community benefits and success.” Pacificans read that exchange for the first time on Wednesday night, in an attachment.

Schools

Oceana Goes to a Smaller League

Two school stories ran this week that are really one story about enrollment, three months before two districts ask Pacifica for money.

Oceana High School is moving its cross country, basketball, soccer and track programs out of the Peninsula Athletic League and into the Private School Athletic League, because a school of roughly 420 students cannot field competitive teams against public schools several times its size. Principal Maritza Torres, to the Tribune's Horace Hinshaw, who has covered Coastside sports for fifty-five years: “When you're part of a small school, getting athletes for a particular sport while you're competing against other extracurricular activities, then our pool of students becomes smaller and smaller.” About a third of Oceana students play a sport. New athletic director Mac DeVore: “Now we're competing with schools that have kind of a similar pool of students… so that we don't have such a mismatch in team size.”

Two days earlier the San Mateo Daily Journal reported that the Jefferson Union High School District — which runs Oceana and Terra Nova — is carrying a $6.5 million deficit this year, while Superintendent Toni Presta described the budget in an email as “stable.” Terra Nova and Oceana together enrolled 1,163 students last year. Westmoor and Jefferson, in Daly City, enrolled 2,314. The district says no Pacifica campus is a current candidate for closure.

JUHSD is asking Pacifica for Measure M on November 3: $98 per parcel for ten years, $3.4 million a year, replacing 2018's Measure Y at $58 — a raise, not a renewal, which is precisely what the sole argument against it says. The Pacifica School District is asking for Measure T, $118 per parcel, on top of the $98 Measure EE already levies. Both need two-thirds.

One long-running question closed this week. The Tribune reports that the recall campaign against school board members Laverne Villalobos and Elizabeth Bredall — which we have carried as unverified since our first edition — “fell short of the signatures needed by the filing deadline.” It did not qualify. Both women are on the November ballot anyway, in a ten-candidate field for four seats, four of whom are running as a slate called Let's Get to Work. One candidate, Tracey Simmons, is campaigning on posting board minutes within forty-eight hours. Given what follows, she may have found the winning issue.

Pier Watch

Closed 78 days · 70 days since the city last mentioned it publicly · 3,864 days of continuous coastline emergency

The Governor has not acted. There is no open FY26 notice.

The county did act, on August 11, extending its own local emergency over the pier by consent, Resolution No. 082038, moved by Supervisor Ray Mueller. Next county review is around October 10.

Monday's consent calendar carries the thirty-day continuation of the pier emergency for what is, by our count, the fourth time since June, alongside the separate coastline emergency that has run since January 22, 2016. Section 22050 now requires the council to review the Power Engineering contract at every regular meeting until it ends. The pier is a permanent agenda item about a structure the city has begun writing about in the past tense.

The Unit of Distance

Eight Days of Whales

Humpbacks came in close off Linda Mar and Rockaway on eight of the last nine days. The volunteer whale-sighting forum logged more than thirty-five reports between August 14 and this morning, most from two regulars posting at dawn. There were bottlenose dolphins off Thornton State Beach on Sunday evening and five more off Mussel Rock on Saturday.

Pacificans described where the whales were using the only measuring system the town has ever agreed on. “730am thur 8/20 whale lunging 1/2 pier length north side of pier.” “hump whale 1.5 pierr lengths out from Nick's Restaurant at Rockaway Beach.” “Humpback whale four pier lengths out at Linda marvich.” “Wales at Linda Mar Beach near Taco Bell.”

One observer stayed at the rail on Thursday morning and filed as he went:

“8:08 a.m. The whale has moved a little South. It's actually just north of the pier now. Little bit less than 1 pier length out and it has been doing fairly frequent lunging now.”

“8:13 a.m. The whale is now barely north of the pier… Still doing some lunging so I'm not sure if it's going to go south or just hang out here but I need to head home now. This whale's been pretty active.”

Seventy-eight days closed, one contractor hired to consider removing part of it, and Pacifica is still measuring the ocean in pier lengths. The typos are theirs. Keep them.
